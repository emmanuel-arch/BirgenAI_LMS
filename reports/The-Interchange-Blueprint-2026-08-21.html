01

The argument in one page

Six systems are live. The next thing to build is not a seventh system — it is the road between companies.

The Micro-Eazy suite works because of one discipline: nothing was migrated. The connected-suite architecture computed the joins rather than copying the data, and it held — 93,376 rows of CollectionTracker joined to Loans with zero orphans, across two databases nobody had ever put in the same query. That is the entire thesis of this document, widened from two databases inside one company to ten companies inside one ecosystem.

The Interchange is the data layer that widens it. Ten lenders already running on infrastructure you administer, each holding a book the others cannot see, all of them lending to overlapping populations in the same city. None of them knows when a borrower who is current with them took a fourth loan somewhere else last Tuesday. The Interchange makes that one fact knowable — in under half a second, without any lender surrendering a borrower record to anyone.

What is being proposed

A two-plane architecture, following the X-Road model for the transactional plane and a modern lakehouse for the analytical one. Real-time member-to-member queries are brokered, signed and logged, and never pooled. Analytical data is tokenised inside each member's own perimeter before it moves, so the shared lake holds no identity that can be reversed. The launch product is Interchange Exposure — an instant multi-lender exposure check, offered on reciprocity: you may query only what you contribute to.

What it costs

Roughly USD 60–90 per month in infrastructure for the entire ecosystem, because the design leans on Cloudflare R2's zero egress fees, a single-binary Kafka-compatible broker, and query engines that are processes rather than clusters. The expensive parts of this project are governance and law, not compute.

Read this before anything else

Six production SQL Servers in this estate accept connections from the public internet today, and nine production credentials sit in cleartext in a git-tracked appsettings.json. Both must be closed before a single outside company is invited in. Section 3 sets out the order of work. This is not a footnote to the plan — it is Phase 0, and it is also the most persuasive thing you can offer a prospective member: we will take your database off the public internet, and give you a network score in return.

Three findings

Finding 1 · Exposed surface

Six SQL Servers complete a TCP handshake from the open internet: 213.148.17.198, 213.148.17.228, 45.150.188.26, 102.214.69.233, 102.220.22.84 and 130.49.168.109. Notably 213.148.17.54 — the busiest connection string in the codebase — is already closed and reached over the tailnet. That is the pattern to replicate across the other six.

Finding 2 · Credentials in version control

ServiceSuite-Portal/ServiceSuite/appsettings.json lines 10–31 hold nine production connection strings in cleartext, commented in and out by hand to switch tenants. Every one of those passwords must be treated as compromised and rotated. Tenant selection belongs in the existing encrypted OrgIntegration.configEnc vault, which the LMS already implements — the pattern exists, it simply has not reached this file.

Finding 3 · The laptop is a single point of failure

All six live systems relay through arch-bishop via Tailscale Funnel. Ten member organisations cannot depend on your workstation being awake. vmi3298281 is already on the tailnet, already serving :80/:443, and carries no database — it is the obvious permanent home for the relay and for the Interchange Registry.

03

Phase 0 — securing the estate

Nothing in this blueprint may begin until the estate is defensible. This work is unglamorous, takes about two weeks, and is a precondition for every conversation with a prospective member.

Each server follows the same four steps, in this order, so that connectivity is proven before exposure is removed:

  1. Join the tailnet. Install Tailscale, tag the node tag:interchange-db, and confirm it appears in tailscale status.
  2. Prove the private path. Repoint one non-critical application to the 100.x address and confirm a successful query. The Micromart cutover from 102.210.148.124 to 100.72.35.56 is the worked example — follow it exactly.
  3. Close the public port at the host firewall and at the provider's edge. Re-run the reachability probe from off-network to confirm the handshake now fails.
  4. Rotate the credential that was previously exposed, and move it into the encrypted vault.

04

Architecture — the two planes

X-Road is a transaction broker, not a data platform. Understanding what it does not do is what makes this design correct.

X-Road routes signed, timestamped, access-controlled request/response messages between organisations. It has no opinion about analytics, no bulk transfer, no streaming, no storage. Most teams that adopt it try to make it carry their data warehouse and fail. The Interchange therefore separates two planes that must never be conflated:

  • Plane A — the road. Real-time, member-to-member, brokered queries. Follows the X-Road model. Nothing is pooled; each answer is computed live by the member who owns the data.
  • Plane B — the lakehouse. Asynchronous, tokenised, analytical. Feeds scoring, benchmarking and early warning. Holds no reversible identity.

4.1  The tokenisation boundary

This is the decision the whole design rests on, and it is where most shared-data projects quietly fail. If each member tokenises with its own secret, tokens never match and exposure is impossible. If all members share one common secret, then a single compromised member can take the secret offline and enumerate every national ID in Kenya — the identifier space is small enough to brute-force in hours. Both of the obvious answers are wrong.

The Interchange uses an oblivious pseudo-random function (OPRF, RFC 9497 over ristretto255). The member blinds the identifier locally and sends the blinded point to the Registry; the Registry applies the ecosystem secret and returns the result; the member unblinds it to obtain the token. The Registry never learns the identifier, and the member never learns the secret — so no participant can compute tokens offline, and the Registry rate-limits every member's token issuance.

member registry ────── ──────── id = "12345678" r = random scalar P = H(id) * r ──── blinded ───► Q = P * k (k = ecosystem secret) ◄─── evaluated ── token = Q * r⁻¹ = H(id) * k registry learns nothing about id · member never sees k offline enumeration impossible · issuance is rate-limited and logged
Why this matters commercially

This is the difference between a product and a liability. Under the Data Protection Act 2019 a tokenised identifier that no participant can reverse is materially easier to defend than a pooled register of borrower identities — and it is the argument that will persuade a competitor to join a network operated by a company that also builds lending software.

05

Interchange Exposure — the launch product

“Is this borrower, who looks clean to me, currently servicing four other loans across this ecosystem?”

Kenyan credit reference bureau data is expensive per query and lags by weeks. The Interchange answers the same question from live books in under half a second, and answers it free at the point of use to any member who contributes. That asymmetry — instant and live versus slow and paid — is what makes lenders join.

5.1  How a query travels

  1. The lender's origination system calls its own node: POST /exposure with national ID, MSISDN and a consent reference.
  2. The node obtains subject_token via the OPRF exchange with the Registry.
  3. The node checks the Bloom filter digests — every member publishes a filter of its active-borrower tokens to R2 every fifteen minutes. Typically this narrows a ten-member fan-out to one or three real calls.
  4. Signed queries go only to members with a probable hit, over mTLS, with a 250 ms timeout each and full parallelism.
  5. Each responding member returns aggregates only — count of active loans, outstanding band, worst arrears bucket, most recent disbursement date. Never a borrower record. Never the lender's identity, unless both parties have enabled mutual disclosure.
  6. The requesting node aggregates, timestamps, writes to its message log, and returns. Every queried member receives an audit entry.
POST /exposure → { national_id, msisdn, consent_ref } { "subject_token": "a4f1…", // never reversible "as_of": "2026-08-21T09:14:22Z", "active_loans": 4, "lenders": 3, "outstanding_band": "50k–100k", // banded, not exact "worst_bucket": "watch_2", "newest_disbursement": "2026-08-19", "velocity_14d": 2, // ← the signal that matters "partial": false, "responded": 3, "queried": 3 }
The design detail worth defending

Bloom filter pre-screening is not only a latency optimisation. Without it, every exposure query broadcasts to all ten members that a named token is being evaluated — which leaks a member's origination pipeline to its competitors. Filtering locally means most members never learn the query happened at all.

Reciprocity is the business model

Contribution and consumption are bound in the policy engine, not in a contract clause. A member who stops publishing stops being able to query, automatically, within one configuration cycle. This is the mechanism that has made every successful contributory bureau work, and it is the reason a network of competitors can be stable.

6.1  On Kafka

Kafka is the correct long-term answer and the reason the wire protocol is non-negotiable — but running a Kafka cluster for ten members' change feeds is operational cost with no return at this stage. Redpanda speaks the same protocol from one binary. When throughput or membership justifies the cluster, the migration is a broker swap, not a rewrite. Choosing the protocol now and deferring the cluster is the discipline; adopting the cluster now would be the mistake.

6.2  On X-Road

The decision taken is to implement X-Road's model — member and subsystem identity, mutually authenticated transport, signed messages, timestamped append-only logs, a central registry distributing signed global configuration — as a lightweight Go node, rather than deploying the reference implementation. The reference implementation requires a Central Server, a Security Server per member, and a trust-services estate (CA, OCSP responder, time-stamping authority) that is disproportionate for ten lenders.

The constraint that makes this reversible is the envelope: keep the message format and identity scheme X-Road-compatible from the first commit. Any member who later demands the certified implementation can then run a real Security Server against the same ecosystem, and the migration is a deployment question rather than a redesign.

Ten companies will not all have the same capability. The node supports four ingestion tiers, and a member can start at the bottom and climb.

A member joining follows the same sequence regardless of tier: certificate issuance and registration in the Registry; deployment of the node inside their perimeter; mapping their schema to the canonical model through a versioned data contract held in git; a shadow period where they contribute but cannot query; then activation.

The shadow period is not optional

Two to four weeks of contribution before query rights are granted does two things: it proves the member's feed is accurate before anyone relies on it, and it establishes reciprocity as a lived norm rather than a clause. Members who query from day one never build the contribution habit.

08

Law, governance, and the risk that could stop this

Unresolved — obtain counsel before Phase 2

Kenya regulates the sharing of credit information between institutions. The Credit Reference Bureau Regulations made under the Banking Act govern bureau activity, and a service that tells one lender about another lender's exposure may be construed as falling within that regime. This is the single largest risk to the plan, and it is a question for a Kenyan financial services lawyer, not for an architecture document.

Three paths are worth putting to counsel: operating strictly as a data processor acting on documented borrower consent on behalf of each member; licensing through or partnering with an already-licensed bureau; or seeking written guidance from the regulator before launch. Resolve this before building Plane A, not after.

Data protection

Registration with the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner is required, and the controller/processor determination must be made deliberately — the tokenisation boundary in §4.1 is what makes the processor argument credible. Borrower consent must be captured at origination, referenced by every query, and revocable; the consent registry in the Registry component exists for this.

Who operates it

BirgenAI operating the Interchange while also supplying lending software to some members is a conflict a competitor will name in the first meeting. Consider a separate legal entity with member representation on its governing body. It costs little now and is very hard to retrofit once ten companies have joined something you own outright.

Residency

R2 offers no Nairobi region. If any member or the regulator requires in-country residency for tokenised data, the fallback is a self-hosted MinIO cluster on Kenyan infrastructure behind the same Iceberg interface — a swap the architecture already permits, at meaningfully higher cost. Establish this requirement before committing to R2, not after loading a terabyte.

Move off the ServiceSuite domain early

Launching on servicesuitecloud.com is right — it borrows credibility you have already earned. But a lender is being asked to join a network that appears, by its domain, to belong to a company that sells to its competitors. Neutral branding is not cosmetic here; it is part of the trust architecture, and it should follow the same timeline as the governance entity in §8.

11

Decisions still open

  1. Regulatory position. Counsel on the CRB Regulations question in §8. Blocking for Phase 2.
  2. Operating entity. BirgenAI subsidiary, or a member-governed association? Affects every membership conversation, so decide before member three.
  3. Data residency. Is in-country storage a hard requirement for any member or regulator? Determines R2 versus self-hosted MinIO. Blocking for Phase 3.
  4. Founding cohort. Which three of the ten interested companies go first? Recommend the two already on the tailnet plus one genuine outsider — an ecosystem that only works for your own clients proves nothing.
  5. Commercial model. Reciprocity governs access, but something must fund operations. Flat membership fee, per-query above a contribution-linked free tier, or bundled into ServiceSuite licensing?
  6. Node ownership. Does the member run the node on their hardware, or do you host it for them? Materially changes the trust story and the support burden.
Recommended next step

Begin Phase 0 immediately — it needs none of the decisions above, it removes the estate's worst risk, and every hour of it is work you would have to do regardless of whether the Interchange is ever built. Put the regulatory question to counsel in the same week.
